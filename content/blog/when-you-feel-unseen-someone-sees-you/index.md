There is a particular ache to feeling invisible. Not hated, not rejected in any dramatic way, just... unnoticed. You sit in a room full of people and no one asks how you are doing. You pour yourself out for others and no one seems to notice the cost. You carry something heavy and no one stops long enough to see it. That kind of invisibility is quiet, and quiet things can go a long time without being named.

But there is a woman in the oldest pages of Scripture who knew that ache, and what happened to her there in the wilderness is worth sitting with.

## A Woman Nobody Talked About

Hagar was not the hero of her story, at least not according to the people around her. She was an Egyptian servant, property in the legal sense, caught in the middle of someone else's family drama. When things fell apart between her and Sarai, she ran. Alone, pregnant, afraid, she found herself by a spring in the wilderness with nowhere to go.

An angel found her there and asked a simple question: where have you come from, and where are you going?

It is the kind of question that can undo a person. Because sometimes the honest answer is: I do not know. I do not know how I got here, and I cannot see a way forward.

What happened next is the part that stays with you. After the angel spoke, after she received the word she needed, Hagar did something remarkable. She gave God a name. No one else in all of Scripture gives God a name. But Hagar did. She called him El Roi, which means the God who sees me.

"And she called the name of Jehovah that spake unto her, Thou art a God that seeth" (Genesis 16:13, ASV).

## What It Means to Be Seen

Being seen by God is not the same as being watched. Anyone can be watched. Security cameras watch people. Algorithms track people. Being seen is different. It means being known, being held in regard, being worth noticing.

Hagar was not a person of status. She had no advocate, no family nearby, no social standing that would cause anyone to come looking for her. And yet God came looking. The God of the universe stopped at a spring in the wilderness for a servant woman who had run away, and he said, in effect, I see you. I see where you are, I see what you are carrying, and I have not forgotten you.

That is the thing about El Roi. He sees the ones that others overlook.

## When Invisibility Feels Like Abandonment

One of the cruelest tricks loneliness plays is convincing you that being unseen by people means being unseen by God. It does not. In fact, Scripture suggests something closer to the opposite. The people who found God most intimately were often the ones who had been pushed to the margins. Hagar in the wilderness. David hiding in caves. The disciples in a locked room, afraid.

Invisibility to the world is not invisibility to God.

You may not receive a dramatic sign. You may not hear a voice or see an angel. But the same God who stopped for Hagar in the desert is paying attention to you right now, in your ordinary, unwitnessed moment. He sees the grief you have not told anyone about. He sees the effort that goes unacknowledged. He sees the longing underneath the fine you tell everyone you are.

## The Name That Holds You

Hagar walked out of that wilderness with something she did not have when she ran into it: a name for the God who had met her there. El Roi. The God who sees.

You can carry that name too. Not as a tidy answer to the pain of being overlooked, but as a true thing to hold onto when the ache of invisibility comes back. And it will come back, because this is ordinary human life, and ordinary human life includes seasons of feeling like no one is looking.

But someone is looking. He was looking at a frightened woman by a spring in the wilderness, and he is looking at you.

"Casting all your anxiety upon him, because he careth for you" (1 Peter 5:7, ASV).

That care is not theoretical. It is for you, in this moment, seen.
